Chapter One

What is the
Palmyra Palm Tree?

Rising majestically across the plains of South India, the Palmyra palm — Borassus flabellifer — is one of humanity's oldest companions. It has witnessed civilisations rise and fall, yet stands unbowed, unyielding, and endlessly generous.

🌍 Found in 12+ countries 📏 Up to 30 metres tall 🕰️ Lifespan: 100+ years 🌱 Zero agricultural inputs 🌳 Tamil Nadu's State Tree

The Palmyra palm (Borassus flabellifer), known as Panai Maram in Tamil, is a tall, robust, fan-leaved palm native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia. It belongs to the family Arecaceae and is one of the most ancient and versatile plants known to mankind.

The tree typically grows 20–30 metres tall with a straight grey trunk marked by leaf-scar rings. Its large, fan-shaped leaves — known as talipot — spread 3 metres wide at the crown. The Palmyra is remarkably drought-tolerant, capable of surviving in regions that receive as little as 400mm of annual rainfall, asking nothing from the land it inhabits.

The tree bears fruit only after 15–20 years but once it begins, it produces abundantly for the remainder of its century-long life. It is dioecious — separate male and female trees — and requires no irrigation, no pesticides, and no fertiliser. It is, quite literally, nature's most perfect gift to a warming world.

"The Palmyra is not just a tree — it is a complete civilisation encoded in wood, fibre, and fruit."

— Ancient Tamil saying

Chapter Two

What is the
Palmyra Palm Fruit?

Beneath the palm's fibrous crown grows one of nature's most remarkable fruits — known variously as the ice apple, nungu, or tadgola. Cooling, nourishing, and otherworldly in texture.

🫐 Palmyra Fruit — Nungu / Ice Apple

The Palmyra fruit (Borassus flabellifer) grows in dense clusters near the crown of the female tree. Each fruit is roughly the size of a coconut — dark brownish-black outside, containing 1–3 translucent, jelly-like seeds known as nungu (Tamil) or ice apples. The flesh is 87% water, cooling, and mildly sweet — a natural electrolyte drink in solid form.

When young and tender, the liquid interior — Padhaneer — is collected as a naturally fermenting sap rich in sugars. When allowed to mature, the fruit yields Panangkarkandu (palm candy / palm jaggery), one of the most nutritious natural sweeteners on earth, with a glycaemic index far lower than refined sugar.

Crucially for Enn Panai, the outer husk of the fruit — typically discarded as agricultural waste after the flesh is consumed — contains extraordinary long-staple fibres. These fibres, extracted through our proprietary process, form the world's first apparel-grade palmyra textile.

🧊
Ice Apple (Nungu)
The translucent, jelly-like seed of the young fruit. 87% water. A summer superfood — naturally cooling and hydrating.
🍯
Palm Jaggery
Unrefined sugar from the sap. Rich in iron, calcium, and minerals. Low glycaemic index — the diabetic-friendly natural sweetener.
🌾
Fruit Fibre
The outer husk yields long-staple fibres with natural lustre and exceptional tensile strength — Enn Panai's breakthrough raw material.
🌱
Germinating Seed
The inner seed, when buried, germinates into a new Palmyra sapling — completing a circular, regenerative supply chain.

Chapter Three

The Palmyra in
Tamil Literature & Epics

For over two thousand years, Tamil poets, philosophers, and saints have honoured the Palmyra palm — weaving it into the very fabric of their wisdom. Its presence in Tamil literature is not incidental; it is foundational.

"பனைமரம் போல் உயர்ந்தவர் தாழ்ந்து வாழ்வது
அரிது; ஆனால் அவர் தரும் நிழல் என்றும் நிலைக்கும்."

"Like the Palmyra that stands tall, the great seldom stoop — yet the shade they cast endures forever."

Classical Tamil Wisdom
📜
Sangam Literature (300 BCE – 300 CE)
The ancient Sangam texts — Akananuru, Purananuru, and Kalithogai — frequently reference the Palmyra as a symbol of strength, endurance, and giving. The Karunilam (landscape classification) of Marutham includes the Palmyra as a boundary tree. Warriors decorated themselves with Palmyra-fibre garlands as symbols of courage.
✍️
Thirukural by Thiruvalluvar (c. 200 BCE)
In Kural 1198, Thiruvalluvar compares the height of longing and love to the height of the Palmyra — no other tree in Tamil literature serves as a metaphor for both physical grandeur and emotional depth simultaneously. The tree's very silhouette became a symbol of aspiration.
🎭
Silappathikaram (2nd century CE)
The epic poem by Ilango Adigal describes festive scenes in Madurai where Palmyra leaves are woven into decorative arches. The tree features in descriptions of village life, harvest celebrations, and the rhythmic sound of Palmyra-fibre instruments during harvest.
🏛️
Temple Inscriptions & Manuscripts
Centuries of Tamil knowledge — Siddha medicine, astronomy, philosophy — were inscribed on Palmyra leaf manuscripts (Ola Chuvadi). The British Museum, Chennai's Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, and the Saraswathi Mahal Library in Thanjavur hold thousands of such manuscripts — the Palmyra leaf as the world's earliest paper.
🎵
Thevaram & Divya Prabandham
The 7th–9th century Saivite hymns of the Nayanmars reference groves of Palmyra trees as sacred spaces. Thirumangai Azhwar in the Divya Prabandham describes the Palmyra-lined shores of Sirkazhi — one of the 108 Divya Desams — as a land blessed by the divine.
👑
Chola, Pandya & Chera Kingdoms
The Palmyra was the royal insignia of the Pandya Kingdom — featured on their battle standards and coins. Pandya warriors were called Panavar (people of the Palm). The tree's image graced temples, palace gates, and manuscripts as a symbol of sovereign power and Tamil identity.

"The Palmyra serves man from root to crown, from seed to fruit, from leaf to bark — it is the only tree in nature that has no waste, no excess, and no silence."

— From classical Tamil folk tradition

Chapter Four

800 Uses of the
Palmyra Palm

Ancient Tamil culture documented 801 uses of the Palmyra palm — a number so extraordinary it earned the tree the title Kalpa Vriksha (the wish-fulfilling tree). Modern science is only beginning to catch up.

🧵
Textile Fibre
Fruit husk fibres spun into luxury apparel-grade yarn. Enn Panai's world-first innovation.
🍯
Palm Jaggery
Mineral-rich natural sweetener from fermented sap. Low GI alternative to refined sugar.
🍹
Neera & Toddy
Fresh palm sap (Neera) — nutritious, non-alcoholic. Fermented into toddy, a traditional beverage.
🍽️
Edible Shoots
Tender germinating seeds (Panai Kizhangu) eaten boiled or raw — a seasonal delicacy.
🧺
Leaf Crafts
Fan-shaped leaves woven into baskets, mats, hats, fans, toys, and ceremonial decorations.
📝
Palm Leaf Manuscripts
Dried leaves as writing material for 2,000+ years of Tamil knowledge, poetry, and science.
🏗️
Timber & Construction
The trunk — extremely hard and termite-resistant — used in construction, bridges, and furniture.
🌿
Medicine (Siddha)
Roots, sap, fruit, and seed used in Siddha medicine for liver health, anaemia, and cooling treatments.
🔥
Biomass Fuel
Leaf fronds and trunk segments used as smokeless fuel in rural communities.
🪣
Containers & Vessels
Young leaf sheaths naturally form waterproof containers used for sap collection and food storage.
🌱
Carbon Sequestration
Each tree sequesters CO₂ across 100 years, making Palmyra groves net-carbon-negative ecosystems.
🎨
Art & Culture
The royal symbol of Pandya kings — featured in temple carvings, coins, and Tamil folk art for millennia.

Chapter Five

Benefits — For People,
Planet & Future

💚
Environmental Benefits
Carbon negative: Each tree absorbs more carbon than it emits over its 100-year life.

Zero-input farming: Requires no water, no pesticides, no fertiliser — it sustains itself.

Biodiversity: Palmyra groves host hundreds of bird, insect, and reptile species.

Soil protection: Deep roots prevent erosion in drought-prone regions.
❤️
Health Benefits
Cooling properties: The fruit is a natural coolant — used in Siddha medicine to treat heat stroke.

Nutritional value: Rich in iron, zinc, potassium, vitamins A, B, and C.

Low glycaemic index: Palm jaggery is safe for diabetics — a healthier sweetener.

Digestive health: Neera (sap) aids digestion and liver function.
💰
Economic Benefits
Livelihoods: A single tree supports 3–4 family members across its productive life.

Multiple income streams: Fruit, sap, leaves, fibre, and timber each generate independent income.

Women's empowerment: Traditionally managed by women — a pathway to independent economic identity.

Export potential: Global premium for natural sustainable fibres and palm-derived foods.
🏘️
Social & Cultural Benefits
Food security: The fruit provides nutrition during summer droughts when other crops fail.

Heritage preservation: Reviving the Palmyra economy reconnects communities with their cultural identity.

Intergenerational wealth: A tree planted today benefits the children of the planter's grandchildren.

Rural migration reversal: Strong Palmyra economies reduce pressure on urban migration.

Chapter Six

How the World
Perceives the Palmyra

Far beyond the shores of India, the Palmyra palm holds sacred status — as food, as fibre, as cultural anchor — across a dozen nations spanning three continents.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka
The Palmyra is the cultural symbol of the Tamil-speaking Northern Province. Toddy-tapping is a centuries-old tradition. Sri Lanka has active government programmes to protect Palmyra groves from post-war land conversion.
🇲🇲
Myanmar
Known as Tan Palm, it is the national tree of Myanmar. Palm jaggery is a staple ingredient and the palm sap wine (Htan Ye) is central to cultural festivals. Myanmar has large-scale plantation programmes.
🇰🇭
Cambodia
The Palmyra is Cambodia's national tree and appears on their national emblem. Palm sugar (Skar Tnaot) is the sweetener of choice. Cambodian artisans craft intricate Palmyra-leaf goods for export.
🇳🇬
Nigeria & West Africa
Palmyra palm wine is a significant cultural and economic commodity across West Africa. The leaves are woven into roofing, baskets, and mats — industries that employ millions.
🇹🇭
Thailand
Thai cuisine features Palmyra fruit extensively. Palm sugar is a premium export. Thailand has developed Palmyra-based cosmetics and food industries worth hundreds of millions of dollars annually.
🌍
Global Fashion Industry
Following Future Fabrics Expo London 2023, global luxury brands are actively sourcing carbon-negative natural fibres. Palmyra fibre — as demonstrated by Enn Panai — is now on the radar of Europe's most prestigious fashion houses.

"In Cambodia, the Palmyra palm is so central to life that a person who does not know its uses is considered uneducated."

— Cambodian Proverb

Chapter Seven

How India is
Acting to Save the Tree

The Palmyra population in Tamil Nadu has declined dramatically over the past century — from an estimated 10 crore trees to fewer than 2 crore today. Both state and central governments are now responding with urgency.

🌳

Tamil Nadu State Tree Status

The Palmyra was declared Tamil Nadu's State Tree — a recognition of its deep cultural and ecological significance. This designation provides legal protections and mandates state conservation programmes.

📋

Tamil Nadu Toddy Policy Reform

The Tamil Nadu government has reformed toddy-tapping policies to make the industry more viable for climbers, incentivising communities to protect existing trees rather than fell them for short-term gain.

🌱

Mass Plantation Drives

Under the Green Tamil Nadu Mission, the state government has launched programmes to plant millions of Palmyra saplings along roadsides, canal banks, and agricultural boundaries — reversing decades of deforestation.

🔬

ICAR & Agricultural University Research

The Indian Council of Agricultural Research and Tamil Nadu Agricultural University have active programmes studying Palmyra varieties, propagation techniques, and value-chain development to increase commercial viability.

🏭

MSME & Startup India Support

Government schemes support Palmyra-based enterprises through MSME loans, technology upgradation grants, and market linkage programmes — recognising the tree's potential as a sustainable livelihood driver.

📱

Digital Initiatives & GI Tags

Efforts are underway to obtain Geographical Indication (GI) tags for Tamil Palmyra products — Palm jaggery from Tirunelveli, Palmyra-craft from Thanjavur — protecting artisans and increasing product value.

Chapter Eight

How You Can
Help Save the Palmyra

Every Palmyra tree saved is a century of shade, carbon capture, and community livelihood secured. Here is how individuals, communities, and brands can contribute to this vital mission.

01

Plant a Palmyra Sapling

Contact your local panchayat, agricultural extension centre, or organisations like Enn Panai to adopt a Palmyra sapling. A single tree you plant today will outlive you and your children — providing value for a century.

02

Choose Palmyra-Derived Products

Every time you buy palm jaggery over refined sugar, ice apple over packaged drinks, or Enn Panai fibre fabric over synthetic textiles — you create economic demand that makes the Palmyra worth protecting.

03

Support Artisan Communities

Purchase Palmyra-leaf baskets, mats, and crafts directly from artisans. Platforms like GoCoop, Craftsvilla, and direct NGO channels connect buyers to traditional craftspeople who depend on the tree's continued existence.

04

Spread Awareness

Share articles, documentaries, and social media content about the Palmyra. Many people outside Tamil Nadu — and indeed outside South India — are entirely unaware of this remarkable tree. Awareness is the first step to conservation.

05

Invest in Sustainable Innovation

Support companies like Enn Panai that are building economic value chains around the Palmyra. When innovation makes the tree commercially viable at scale, conservation becomes self-sustaining.

06

Demand Sustainability from Brands

Ask the fashion brands you buy from: what is your fibre sourced from? Is it carbon-negative? When consumers demand natural, traceable fibres, brands will respond — creating market pull for Palmyra fibre at global scale.
